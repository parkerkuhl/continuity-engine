**Chapter 2: Grey at the Picket Line**

Morning came up brown off the Rhoyne. Smoke from the cookfires lay flat over the camp and wouldn't lift, and the Black Wall stood above it all like it had been there before the river and meant to be there after.

Marro woke with his right hand throbbing in its splints. Two fingers, bound straight to a strip of ash wood, and they itched like the bones were knitting with ants. He flexed what he could. The thumb worked. The rest of the hand was an opinion.

Four silver stags in his purse. He'd counted them twice in the dark. Counting didn't make more of them.

Rickard was already up, sitting on an upturned grain cask outside the command tent with his left arm strapped across his chest. He was trying to buckle his sword belt one-handed and losing. The burn scar on his jaw went white when he clenched it.

"Squire," he said, without looking up. "If you're going to stand there, stand there holding this."

Marro took the buckle. Rickard's shirt hung open at the collar, and a leather cord showed against his throat, dark with sweat. Rickard tugged the shirt shut over it with his good hand, quick, the way a man brushes off a fly.

"Malaquo's barges are late," Rickard said. "Third day. The men will start eating their boots by the week's end, and then they'll start eating the paymaster." A dry twitch of the mouth. "I'd sell tickets."

Down the line, Sergeant Hesse was crouched by Ash's foreleg, her patch turned toward the camp so she could watch the horse with her good eye. She ran a hand down the swollen cannon bone and swore at it, long and inventive, by the Black Wall and several things lower. Ash shifted his weight off the leg and blew.

Then Marro saw them.

Two men in grey at the edge of the picket line, where the camp followers sold onions and bad wine. Not buying. Just standing, hands folded, watching the command tent the way crows watch a cart that's about to tip. One of them caught Marro's eye and lifted two fingers to his brow, polite as a priest.

Pallo's men. Four days early, and in the company's own camp.

Marro's stomach went small and cold. Behind him, Rickard grunted as the buckle bit home.

"Friends of yours?" Rickard said. He'd seen them too. Rickard saw most things.

Hesse had stopped swearing at the horse.

The grey men didn't follow. When Marro looked back from the horse lines they were still at the onion carts, hands folded, as if they had all the time in the world. They had four days of it, anyway.

"Squire!" Jory Pike waved him in, a gap-toothed Tyroshi boy two years older than Marro and twice as loud. "Look who's still got one good hand. Sit, sit. Cask's warm."

Around the far barrel, five of the younger sellswords hunched over a game of hazard. Coppers and a few stags lay in a ragged pot. A big Westerosi called Dunk Two-Stones was holding the bones in his fist and blowing on them like they owed him money.

"Pot's at nineteen," Jory said. "Dunk's on a cold run. Three throws, he's bled seven stags. Somebody's got to take it off him before the Braavosi does." He nodded at a lean, quiet man in the corner with a ring on every finger, who smiled at nothing.

Nineteen stags. Marro did the sum without wanting to. Nineteen and his four made twenty-three. Seven short of Pallo. Close enough to taste.

His purse sat heavy against his hip. Four coins. He could feel each one.

He could feel the other thing, too. Hesse's face when he'd sworn it, three weeks back, her one eye fixed on him like a crossbow. *Never again, Sergeant. By the Black Wall.* She'd said nothing, just nodded once, and somehow that had been worse than any oath she could have thrown at him.

The splints itched.

Dunk threw. The bones skittered across the barrelhead, clicked off a copper, and came up a two and a one. The lean-to groaned. Dunk swore and shoved his last coins in.

"Cold, cold, cold," Jory crowed. He slid a pair of bone dice across the barrel toward Marro, yellowed and chipped at one corner. "Next caster's yours if you want it. Minimum's a stag."

Outside, a horse stamped. Through the gap in the sailcloth Marro could see a slice of the picket line, and the onion carts beyond, and nobody in grey. That was almost worse.

The Braavosi with the rings was watching Marro now, still smiling, turning one ring slowly round his thumb.

Somebody pushed the cup at Marro. A battered leather thing, the bones rattling inside it. "One throw won't hurt," said a red-haired lad whose name Marro didn't know. "Stag to cast. You match the caster's point or you don't. Gods do the rest."

Twenty-two. Twenty-two and his four was twenty-six. He'd still be four short of Pallo. Four stags. Pallo might take twenty-six and a promise. Pallo might take twenty-six and his other hand.

Marro took the cup. He hadn't decided anything. His left hand had simply closed on it, and the leather was warm from Dunk's grip.

"House rules," the Braavosi said from his corner. It was the first time he'd spoken. His voice was soft, his Common careful, each word set down like a coin on a counting table. "A man who casts must cover what he wishes to win. You wish the whole pot, you put up the whole pot. Or the part you can cover." He turned his ring. "You have four, I think. Four wins you four."

So that was the game. Four would win four. Eight wouldn't pay Pallo. Twenty-two would need twenty-two up front, and nobody here was going to lend a squire with broken fingers eighteen stags against his own throw.

Unless somebody was.

The Braavosi was still smiling. "Or," he said, "a man might put up something that is not coin."

Jory laughed a little too loudly. Dunk didn't laugh at all. Outside, somebody down the horse lines was calling Marro's name. It sounded like Hesse.
