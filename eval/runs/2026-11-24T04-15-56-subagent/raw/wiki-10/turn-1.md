The big pavilion stank of wet canvas and too many men who'd slept in their mail.

Marro stood behind Rickard's stool with the helm in both hands, the good one doing the work, the splinted fingers just resting on the crest. Rickard sat with his left arm bound against his chest. He hadn't tried to hide it. Nobody in the pavilion was fool enough to mention it either.

The captains took the benches along the table. At the head, Lysono Qhoran had his ledgers open and his sleeves pinned back so the ink wouldn't touch them. He didn't look up when he spoke.

"Two months of grain on the Triarch's credit," he said. "The barges that were to bring the third did not come this morning. And last night, the wall doubled its torches." Now he looked up. "I would like to know how the Triarch knows when to be careful, when we have told him nothing."

Nobody answered. Somebody's bench creaked.

"Someone talks," Lysono said, mild as milk. "I keep the books. I will find the line that doesn't add."

Rickard said nothing. His jaw worked once under the burn scar, and then he was still. Marro watched the back of his neck, where the cord of the strongbox key disappeared under his collar, and couldn't have said why he was watching it.

At the far end of the table, Ser Tobbin pushed himself up. Big man, rust on his gorget, eyes red like he'd been drinking or not sleeping.

"While we're counting," he said, "count my sister. Dagna. She was to meet me three nights back with a boat and she never came." He swept the table with a look and stopped on Marro. "You were in the alleys by the river that night, squire. Half the camp saw you come back with your hand broke. You see her?"

The pavilion went quiet in the way rooms do when everyone has decided to listen without seeming to.

The helm was suddenly very heavy. Marro could feel the river cold again, the weight going over the edge, the splash that wasn't loud enough.

Rickard didn't turn around. Hesse, by the tent pole, shifted her one eye to Marro and kept it there.
