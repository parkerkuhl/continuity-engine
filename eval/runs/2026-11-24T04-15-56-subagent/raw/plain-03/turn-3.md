The sailcloth flap lifted and let in a wedge of brown daylight, and one of the grey men ducked under it.

He wasn't big. He didn't need to be. He was neat in a way nobody in the camp was neat, his grey wool brushed, his boots clean of horse dung, and he sat down on the cask beside Marro as if it had been kept for him. Jory found somewhere else to look. The red-haired lad put his coin back in his purse.

The man smiled. He had very good teeth for a Volantene, and he showed all of them.

"What do you want?" Marro said.

"Want?" The grey man spread his hands, palms up, the way a priest shows he holds no knife. "Nothing, little brother. I am a friend. Pallo is a friend. Friends visit." He glanced at the leather cup in Marro's left hand, then at the splinted right. "Ah. Still the hand. A pity. Pallo was sorry to hear."

He hadn't heard it from Marro.

"Four days," the man went on, pleasant as weather. "Thirty silver stags, at the new moon. Pallo does not forget, so you need not worry about forgetting. This is a kindness." He leaned in. His breath smelled of cloves. "But Pallo is also a reasonable man. A merchant. He knows the price of grain is high in this camp, and the price of silver is higher. So he says to me, Teodo, go and tell the squire there are other coins than stags."

The Braavosi in the corner had stopped turning his ring.

"Your captain," Teodo said softly. "Snow. He keeps a little box in his tent, they say. Iron bands. Pallo would like very much to know what a sellsword captain keeps in a box when he has no land and no wife." He tapped two fingers on the barrelhead, once, twice. "Not take. Only know. A look. A word to me. And thirty stags become ten. Perhaps nothing."

Outside, closer now, Hesse's voice cut through the horse lines. "*Marro!* Gods' teeth, boy, where've you crawled to?"

Teodo didn't turn his head. He kept smiling at Marro, waiting, the dice still rattling faintly in the cup where Marro's hand had begun to shake.
