The grey men didn't follow. When Marro looked back from the horse lines they were still at the onion carts, hands folded, as if they had all the time in the world. They had four days of it, anyway.

The dice house wasn't a house. It was a sailcloth lean-to propped against the farrier's wagon, three barrels for tables and a brazier for light, and it stank of horse piss and cheap Lyseni red. The same place he'd broken his fingers. Somebody had scrubbed the blood off the near barrel, mostly.

"Squire!" Jory Pike waved him in, a gap-toothed Tyroshi boy two years older than Marro and twice as loud. "Look who's still got one good hand. Sit, sit. Cask's warm."

Around the far barrel, five of the younger sellswords hunched over a game of hazard. Coppers and a few stags lay in a ragged pot. A big Westerosi called Dunk Two-Stones was holding the bones in his fist and blowing on them like they owed him money.

"Pot's at nineteen," Jory said. "Dunk's on a cold run. Three throws, he's bled seven stags. Somebody's got to take it off him before the Braavosi does." He nodded at a lean, quiet man in the corner with a ring on every finger, who smiled at nothing.

Nineteen stags. Marro did the sum without wanting to. Nineteen and his four made twenty-three. Seven short of Pallo. Close enough to taste.

His purse sat heavy against his hip. Four coins. He could feel each one.

He could feel the other thing, too. Hesse's face when he'd sworn it, three weeks back, her one eye fixed on him like a crossbow. *Never again, Sergeant. By the Black Wall.* She'd said nothing, just nodded once, and somehow that had been worse than any oath she could have thrown at him.

The splints itched.

Dunk threw. The bones skittered across the barrelhead, clicked off a copper, and came up a two and a one. The lean-to groaned. Dunk swore and shoved his last coins in.

"Cold, cold, cold," Jory crowed. He slid a pair of bone dice across the barrel toward Marro, yellowed and chipped at one corner. "Next caster's yours if you want it. Minimum's a stag."

Outside, a horse stamped. Through the gap in the sailcloth Marro could see a slice of the picket line, and the onion carts beyond, and nobody in grey. That was almost worse.

The Braavosi with the rings was watching Marro now, still smiling, turning one ring slowly round his thumb.
