**Chapter 2: Grey Men**

The fog came up off the Rhoyne before dawn and sat on the camp like wet wool. Marro woke with his right hand throbbing in its splints, the two bad fingers fat and hot, and lay a while listening to the cookfires spit.

Four days. Thirty stags. He had four.

He didn't need to count them again. He counted them anyway.

Rickard was already up, sitting on an upturned grain crate outside the command tent with his left arm strapped to his chest and a cup of something steaming in his right. The burn scar on his jaw looked grey in that light. He watched the fog and didn't look at Marro.

"Malaquo's barges were late," he said. "Third time this month. Go tell Hesse to put six men on the wagon road. If the Triarch wants to starve us he can do it to our faces."

The horse lines stank of wet straw. Hesse stood over Ash with a hoof in her hand and her patch pushed up on her forehead to scratch beneath it, then pulled it back down when she saw him. The mare's off foreleg was wrapped again.

"She's no better," Hesse said. "Gods' teeth, don't look at her like that. Say what he wants."

Marro had his mouth open to say it when someone said his name behind him.

Ser Tobbin came through the fog with his cloak dark at the hem and a long face that had not slept. He was a big man, slow-spoken, with his sister's narrow nose. Marro had not noticed that before. He noticed it now.

"You're the captain's boy. You were in the dock alleys a week back, the night of the brawl. Folk saw you." Tobbin turned his gloves over in his hands. "My sister kept a stall down there. Dagna. She's not been seen since that night. I'm going down to ask after her tonight, and I want someone with me who knows the turnings."

Beyond him, at the edge of the lines where the fog thinned, a man in a grey cloak was leaning on a fence post, doing nothing at all.

Hesse's one eye went from Tobbin to Marro and stayed there.

Tobbin was still waiting on his answer, gloves turning in his hands, when the runner came through the fog.

It was Rickard's tent-boy, Cass, barefoot and out of breath. "Captain wants the squire. Now, he says."

Rickard hadn't moved from the grain crate. The cup was empty. Marro looked past him at the Black Wall, a dark line behind the fog, and the first thing he thought of was the inside of it. Silk awnings. Old Blood eating off silver while the company chewed weevils. Rickard's mother's people, somewhere behind all that fused stone.

Rickard saw where he was looking. His grey eyes went flat.

"Whatever you're chewing on, swallow it," he said, low. "There's no road through that wall for the likes of us. No pass, no bridge, no friends. If there were, I wouldn't be sitting in mud." He tipped the cup over and watched the dregs run out. "And what I told you stays between you and me. Not Hesse. Not anyone."

Then, at his usual volume: "Did you tell her about the wagon road?"

Behind them, near the horse lines, Tobbin stood where Marro had left him, watching. The man in grey had gone from the fence post. Marro didn't see where.

Rickard was waiting.

Hesse left Ash and came across the mud with her bowlegged sergeant's walk. She stopped at Marro's shoulder, close enough that he could smell horse liniment and old smoke on her, and dropped her voice so it went no further than his ear.

"What's the knight want with you? Face on him like a wet funeral."

"I don't know," Marro said.

Her eye stayed on him a beat too long. She'd known him since he was small enough to hide in a grain sack, and she'd seen him lie to dice-house men and quartermasters and once to a Triarch's tax-taker. She knew how it looked.

"Huh," she said. That was all.

Rickard watched them both from the crate. He'd heard none of it, and he didn't like that.

"Sergeant. Six men on the wagon road, Malaquo's barges are late again. Pick ones who can keep their tempers." He looked at Marro. "You. Inside. I want my arm redressed and I want to talk."

The tent flap hung open behind him. Inside, on the camp table, sat the iron-bound strongbox Rickard never let out of his sight.

Tobbin hadn't moved from the edge of the horse lines.
