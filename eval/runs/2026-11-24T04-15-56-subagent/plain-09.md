**Chapter 2: Grey Men**

The camp woke to smoke and mud and somebody's mule screaming.

Marro had slept badly on the splinted hand. The two broken fingers throbbed under the bindings every time his heart beat, which was too often. He kept the hand tucked against his chest under the borrowed cloak, the way a man carries something stolen.

Four silver stags in his purse. Thirty owed. Four days. The arithmetic didn't improve with sleep.

Rickard's tent stood at the end of the captains' row. The captain sat on a stool outside it with his left arm strapped tight across his body, shaving one-handed with a knife and no mirror. The burn scar on his jaw made the work slow. He didn't look up.

"You've a face like a wet funeral," he said. "Olyvar's dead. You're not. Fetch water."

Down by the horse lines Hesse was crouched beside Ash, running her hand down the grey's off foreleg. The patch over her left eye was new leather, still stiff. Ash held the leg off the ground and wouldn't put it down. Hesse spat into the mud and said something about the farrier's mother that would have got a priest's attention, if the company still had one.

Then Marro saw the men by the water barrels.

Two of them. Grey cloaks, grey hoods, standing at their ease among sellswords who didn't know them, as if the camp were a market. One was eating an apple. The other was watching the captains' row. Watching Rickard's tent, near enough.

Pallo's men. Four days early.

The one with the apple found Marro across the yard and lifted it a little, friendly. Then he started walking toward him.

Behind Marro, the captain's voice: "Boy. Water."

The grey man was twenty paces off and in no hurry.

The grey man with the apple had only wanted a look at him. He'd stood close enough for Marro to smell the fruit on his breath, said "Four days, little squire," and wandered off toward the river with his friend. That was yesterday. Now it was three.

The free afternoon came because Rickard's shoulder went hot in the night and the surgeon ordered him to lie still, and a captain lying still has no use for a squire. Marro left with Lissa's letter folded inside his shirt, against his skin, where rain couldn't get at it. He'd read the direction on the outside so many times the words had gone strange. Her mother's name. A street near the dragon fountain. The Dragon's Den.

Which was on the other side of the river.

He knew that before he set out, and he walked anyway, the way a man walks toward a debt.

The Long Bridge was a road with houses on it, shops and shrines crowding both sides, and in peacetime you could cross it and never know you were over water. Not now. At the western end the Triarch's men had built a gate out of carts and timber. Spearmen in tiger-striped enamel stood at it. A clerk sat under an awning with a ledger and a pot of red wax, and every person who came up the line held out a paper and waited while he looked at the stamp.

No paper, no crossing. A fishwife ahead of Marro argued for it and got a spear butt in the ribs for her trouble, not hard, the way you'd move a dog.

Marro had a cudgel, four silver stags, a splinted hand and a letter. None of it had a stamp on it.

The line shuffled forward. Six people ahead of him now. Down at the waterline, under the bridge's first arch, a boy was poling a flat skiff out from the reeds, looking for fares and not looking at the guards.

The clerk dipped his wax. Five ahead.

The house was a mile off and across the water, and the line didn't care how badly he wanted to knock on its door.

Four ahead. Three. The fishwife had gone off cursing toward the river stairs, rubbing her ribs. An old man in a wine-merchant's coat handed up his paper, and the clerk held it to the light, turned it over, pressed a thumbnail to the wax to see if it was fresh. He took his time about it. The old man paid him a coin that wasn't on any tariff, and the spearmen let him through.

So there was a price. Whether four stags covered it, and whether a clerk would take silver from a sellsword's squire with no paper at all, was another matter. The Golden Company was camped outside these people's walls. Marro's borrowed cloak had the company's colour at the hem, faded but there.

Two ahead.

Under the first arch the skiff boy had found no fares and was drifting back toward the reeds, idle, one hand trailing in the brown water. A pair of the Triarch's men watched the river from a balcony over the arch. They didn't seem to be watching him in particular. They didn't seem to be not watching him either.

Lissa's letter was warm against his ribs. Three days to the new moon.

The clerk looked up past the woman in front of Marro and found him with flat, bored eyes.

"Paper," he said, before it was even Marro's turn.

The clerk had already looked past him by the time he left the line. A boy with no paper wasn't worth the breath.

The way back to camp ran through the Fishmarket, all gutted carp and wet stone and gulls fighting over the gutters. Marro was halfway through it when the alley on his left produced a man.

Big. Not tall so much as wide, like a door taken off its hinges and stood up. Grey cloak, grey hood pushed back, a shaved head with a nick in one ear where someone had once tried for more. He didn't block the street. He didn't need to. He just stood near enough that walking past him would mean brushing his sleeve.

"Pallo sends his love," he said. His voice was pleasant and a little hoarse, a tavern singer's voice gone to seed. "He worries. Young men forget things."

Marro asked how long.

The big man smiled as if that were a clever question.

"Three days, little squire. New moon. Thirty silver stags." He held up three thick fingers, then looked pointedly at Marro's splinted hand. "Three. You've two that don't work already. Pallo thinks it would be a shame to make it more. He's sentimental."

He took a step closer. He smelled of cloves and old sweat.

"And he says, if a young man can't find coin, perhaps a young man can find something else worth coin. Things a captain keeps. Things a captain says over his cups." He shrugged. "That's Pallo's thought. Not mine. I only carry messages."

Four silver stags sat in Marro's purse. They felt very light.

The big man waited, patient, blocking nothing.
